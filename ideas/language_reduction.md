# Reducing the Effective Complexity of the Language Data-Generating Process

## The Problem

Language is generated by an extraordinarily complex process. Syntax, semantics, pragmatics, world knowledge, discourse state, speaker intent, and register all interact to produce text. A small language model (say, ~1B parameters) cannot capture all of this structure. It spends much of its capacity on rare phenomena — unusual collocations, garden-path constructions, rare word senses — that contribute little to the clean, algebraically regular structure (like compositional word embeddings) that we often care about studying.

The question is: **can we reduce the complexity of a language dataset so that a small model can learn its core regularities more efficiently, while preserving the properties that make language interesting?**

This is not the same as domain restriction (training on only Wikipedia) or vocabulary reduction (more aggressive BPE). Those approaches recode or subsample the same underlying process without necessarily simplifying it. We want to reduce the number of latent factors a model must represent to explain the data.

## The Core Insight

The complexity of language, as encountered by a learner, does not live in some abstract true data-generating process. It lives in the **effective DGP as instantiated in a finite dataset**. Every training corpus is already a lossy sample of the full complexity of English. A model always learns the dominant regularities first and memorizes the residuals.

This means we can slide the knob. By pushing text toward the **mode** of the language distribution — replacing rare tokens, smoothing unusual constructions, collapsing low-frequency patterns into high-frequency ones — we produce a dataset where fewer regularities explain more of the variance. The residual that needs memorizing becomes smaller and more uniform.

Crucially, the regularities we care about are **high-frequency phenomena by nature**. The king − man + woman = queen analogy emerges from dominant co-occurrence patterns: gendered titles appearing in gendered contexts, thousands of times. This *is* the mode. You would have to denoise absurdly aggressively to destroy it. Meanwhile, the long-tail phenomena being removed — rare senses, unusual register shifts, creative metaphor — are exactly the capacity sinks that obscure clean structure in small models.

## Connection to Neural Scaling Laws

There is strong work arguing that empirical neural scaling laws arise from the **heavy-tailed distribution of subtask difficulties** in language. The model learns phenomena in roughly frequency-ranked order as parameters increase. Each new tranche of capacity buys the next slice of the tail, producing smooth power-law loss curves.

If this picture is correct, then denoising truncates the tail of the subtask distribution. The scaling curve should steepen and plateau earlier — a small model reaches saturation on the simplified distribution much faster. This makes the relationship between model capacity and learned structure far more legible, which is independently valuable for studying how models allocate their representational resources.

## Why Not Synthetic Toy Problems?

The standard alternative for controlled-DGP experiments is synthetic data: modular arithmetic, group composition tables, formal grammars. These offer clean, known DGPs and interpretable circuits, but the regularities are mathematical, not semantic. You will never find compositional meaning in a group theory task.

Denoised language preserves the **semantic compositionality** that makes language uniquely interesting to study — word embeddings with meaningful geometry, selectional preferences, syntactic patterns — while cutting the combinatorial explosion that makes full-scale language intractable for small-model interpretability work. It occupies a useful middle ground: a dataset with a DGP simple enough to reason about, but whose structure is still genuinely linguistic.

## Potential Pitfalls

**Attractor collapse.** Denoising doesn't just remove noise; it can merge nearby-but-distinct modes. Metaphorical and literal uses of "bright" might collapse into one pattern. For interpretability research on small models, this is arguably a feature — fewer, cleaner regularities — but researchers should be aware it's happening.

**Pretrained model bias.** Using a large pretrained model to perform the denoising introduces the model's own inductive biases. It will preferentially preserve structure it represents well (semantic and syntactic regularities) while collapsing structure it represents poorly. The result is the intersection of language's structure with the teacher architecture's biases, not language's structure per se.

## What This Enables

A researcher applying this approach would train small models (~1B parameters or less) on progressively denoised versions of the same base corpus. The resulting models should:

- Saturate earlier on their training distribution, making capacity allocation more legible.
- Exhibit cleaner embedding geometry, with fewer dimensions needed to capture the remaining regularities.
- Be more amenable to mechanistic interpretability, since the space of possible learned structure is smaller and more enumerable.

The tradeoff is explicit: grammatical fluency and expressive coverage degrade, but the structure that remains is more concentrated, more regular, and more interpretable. For toy-scale research into how models learn and represent linguistic structure, that is a favorable trade.

## Operationalization Proposal

### Theoretical grounding: Cagnetta et al. (2026)

Cagnetta et al. ("Deriving neural scaling laws from the statistics of natural language") show that the data-limited scaling exponent α_D = γ/(2β) is fully determined by two measurable dataset statistics:

- **γ**: the exponent governing the power-law decay of conditional entropy H_n with context length n. H_n − H_∞ ≍ n^{−γ}. Intuitively: how fast does knowing more context reduce uncertainty?
- **β**: the exponent governing the power-law decay of token-token correlations ‖C(n)‖_op with temporal separation n. ‖C(n)‖_op ≍ n^{−β}. Intuitively: how fast does statistical dependence between tokens fade with distance?

This framework makes the denoising proposal precise. "Reducing effective DGP complexity" means changing γ and β — specifically, increasing γ (making the remaining patterns more predictable from local context) and reshaping the correlation spectrum so that fewer singular directions capture more of the variance. Cagnetta's theory then gives a quantitative prediction for how the scaling curve should change, with no free parameters.

### Key design decision: context-dependent statistical denoising, no language models

We avoid LM-based rewriting (e.g., having GPT-4 paraphrase the corpus into simpler English). As noted in the Potential Pitfalls section, this conflates language structure with the teacher model's inductive biases — a fatal confound for interpretability research. Instead, we intervene using corpus statistics directly, and use trained models only where the theory demands it (estimating γ).

**β is defined in terms of pairwise token co-occurrence counts** — the two-point covariance matrix C_{μ,ν}(n) = P(X_i=μ, X_{i+n}=ν) − P(X_i=μ)P(X_{i+n}=ν). This is computable from raw corpus counts. Its singular value spectrum separates dominant correlation patterns (top singular vectors: gendered nouns co-occurring with gendered contexts, function words with syntactic frames, etc.) from rare, idiosyncratic co-occurrence patterns (the bottom of the spectrum). We can identify and suppress the latter without any model.

**γ requires a model to measure** (it involves conditional entropy over exponentially many contexts) but not to change. Token replacement that targets the co-occurrence spectrum should increase γ as a side effect, because the remaining patterns become more predictable from local context.

### Why not just frequency filtering?

A natural objection: if the singular value spectrum of C(n) is dominated by token frequency (high-frequency tokens have large entries and land in the top singular vectors), isn't spectral denoising just frequency filtering with extra steps?

At mild τ (replacing only very rare tokens), the answer is roughly yes — spectral participation and frequency are highly correlated. But at aggressive τ (0.5+), the approaches diverge in an important way.

**Frequency filtering is context-independent.** A token either is or isn't below the frequency threshold. "Bank" has one frequency and gets one decision, regardless of whether it appears in "bank account" (a dominant pattern) or "river bank" (a weaker one). This targets the **marginal** distribution of tokens.

**But the DGP of language is a conditional distribution** — p(x_{n+1} | x_{1:n}). That's what the model actually learns from. The multi-lag covariance structure enables **context-dependent** denoising: for each token occurrence at position i, compute how much of its co-occurrence with surrounding context is explained by the top-k subspace versus the residual:

- explained(x, context) = Σ_n C_k(n)[x, x_{i+n}]
- residual(x, context) = Σ_n (C(n) − C_k(n))[x, x_{i+n}]

If the explained fraction is high, this token-in-context participates in a dominant pattern — leave it. If the residual dominates, this token-in-context is unusual — flag it for replacement. The threshold is controlled by τ.

This means τ=0.5 doesn't remove the bottom 50% of tokens by frequency — it removes the bottom 50% of **token-occurrences** by contextual typicality. A common word in an unusual context gets replaced. A rare word in a highly regular context survives. At aggressive τ this distinction matters a lot: context-independent replacement would force polysemous tokens into a single pattern, destroying structure that both senses were individually regular enough to keep.

### The denoising pipeline

Parameterize by a single aggressiveness scalar τ ∈ [0, 1], where τ=0 is the original corpus and τ=1 is completely uniform text.

**Step 1: Compute statistics (subsample, ~hours on CPU).** Take a subsample of ~500M–1B tokens. Compute token frequencies and the co-occurrence matrices C(n) for lags n = 1 to ~50. A coarse vocabulary reduction first (map the bottom ~90% of token types to their most common bigram-context substitute) shrinks V from ~32K to ~3.2K, making each C(n) ~80MB and all 50 lags fit in ~4GB.

**Step 2: Spectral analysis.** For each lag n, compute the top-k SVD of C(n). The singular value spectrum is heavy-tailed: a small number of directions (≲10) capture a large fraction of the total energy. The parameter τ controls what fraction of the spectral energy to keep — at τ=0.5, keep the top singular vectors accounting for 50% of ‖C(n)‖²_F.

**Step 3: Score and replace (one pass, context-dependent).** For each token occurrence at position i, compute its contextual typicality score (explained vs. residual co-occurrence with surrounding context, as described above). If the score falls below the τ-dependent threshold, flag it for replacement. For flagged positions, score candidate replacements using the multi-lag covariance structure: for each candidate y, sum C_k(n) evaluated between y and every token within a context window at the corresponding lag n, in both directions. The token two positions away votes through C(2), the token five positions away votes through C(5), etc. Pick the highest-scoring candidate.

Both the flag-for-replacement decision and the replacement selection are context-dependent. The same token survives in contexts where it participates in dominant patterns and gets replaced in contexts where it doesn't. This is a single pass through the corpus. For each of ~10B positions, the scoring involves ~100 terms (50 lags × 2 directions), each a lookup in a low-rank matrix — ~10^12 operations total, minutes on a GPU. Embarrassingly parallel across shards.

**Important: the denoising operates at the token level, not the word level.** BPE subword fragments like "ly" might seem vulnerable to incoherent replacement, but the context-dependent scoring protects against this. Morphological fragments participate in highly regular co-occurrence patterns with their stems — exactly the kind of dominant structure captured by the top singular vectors. At any given position, "ly" after "positive" will have a high contextual typicality score and won't be flagged. Only token-occurrences that are contextually unusual get replaced.

### Measurement and validation

For each τ value in the sweep:

1. **Recompute β** from co-occurrence counts on the denoised corpus (cheap, same method as Step 1).
2. **Estimate γ** by training small AR models at 4–5 different dataset sizes P and fitting the convergence of the n-gram losses L_n. Cagnetta et al. show γ is architecture-independent, so any reasonable model family works.
3. **Predict** α_D(τ) = γ(τ) / (2β(τ)).
4. **Validate** by training models at ~10 values of P and comparing the empirical scaling exponent to the prediction.

This produces a curve showing how (γ, β, α_D) evolve with denoising aggressiveness. The scientific contribution is not just "denoised data helps small models" (which is obvious) but the quantitative relationship between denoising, measurable language statistics, and scaling behavior — including whether γ and β covary under denoising and what their joint trajectory looks like.

### Compute budget

The denoising itself is cheap. Statistics computation uses a subsample and takes hours on CPU. Corpus transformation is a single-pass token lookup. The dominant cost is the validation: ~1 GPU-week per τ value for the scaling sweep (small models, ~1B params, at multiple dataset sizes). A sweep over 5–10 τ values is ~1–2 GPU-months. Feasible as a research project.

### Long-range structure: truncate, don't denoise

For correlations at large temporal separation (n > ~20), ‖C(n)‖_op is already weak (decaying as n^{−β} with β ≈ 0.9), and a small model on limited data cannot exploit them anyway (the data threshold P_n* = n^{2β} grows steeply). Rather than attempting to denoise long-range structure — which would require a language model — we simply truncate it: cap document length, shuffle at sentence or paragraph boundaries. This is honest about what the small model can actually use.
